Every question in this family shares one move: **accept the premises as true, and attack the connection between the premises and the conclusion.** Challenging a premise directly is always wrong here, the tracker logs that specific mistake as the `premise-challenge` trap, and it's a free elimination once you can spot it.

The organizing idea underneath the whole family is the **argument core**: the conclusion, and the one premise that most directly supports it, with the *gap* between them made explicit, what has to be true for that premise to actually get you to that conclusion.

## Necessary assumption

*"Which one of the following is an assumption on which the argument depends?"*

Find something the argument **requires**, without which it collapses. It doesn't have to be enough to *prove* the conclusion on its own, just something the argument can't survive without.

**The negation test** resolves most hard necessary-assumption questions: negate the answer choice. If the argument falls apart under the negation, that answer is necessary. If the argument still basically works, it isn't. Skipping this test is common enough to have its own tracker tag (`negation-test`), run it deliberately rather than trusting a feel for the answer.

Necessary assumptions tend to be **modest**, they defend against an objection rather than building a whole bridge to the conclusion. An answer with strong language (*all, always, only, guarantees*) is often too strong to actually be necessary, even when it sounds supportive.

## Sufficient assumption

*"...allows the conclusion to be properly drawn" / "if assumed, enables the conclusion to follow logically"*

Find something that, added to the premises, **guarantees** the conclusion, usually a bridge connecting a term in the premises to a term in the conclusion that otherwise never gets linked.

The boundary between necessary and sufficient assumption is the most heavily tested distinction in this family. A sufficient assumption may say far more than the argument strictly needs; a necessary one may say far less than would be needed to fully prove the conclusion. Confusing the two, treating a "may possibly help" answer as sufficient, or a "guarantees everything" answer as necessary, is exactly the `conditional` gap in the tracker vocabulary.

## Flaw

*"most vulnerable to criticism because..." / "error of reasoning" / "fails to consider"*

A flaw question asks you to name the error. Flaws are really just assumptions restated as accusations: the assumption "the sample is representative" becomes the flaw "generalizes from an unrepresentative sample."

Two elimination questions resolve most hard flaw questions:
- **"Did that actually happen?"**, the answer names a real flaw type, but this particular argument doesn't commit it.
- **"So what?"**, the answer accurately describes something the argument does, but that thing isn't actually a flaw.

## Strengthen / weaken

Make the conclusion more, or less, likely, not prove or disprove it outright, since these are questions of degree. New information is fair game and expected. The most common correct-answer shapes: ruling out (or confirming) an alternative cause on a causal argument, confirming or undermining a sample's representativeness, or closing (or widening) the specific gap between the premise's term and the conclusion's term.

A frequent miss: when an argument has two separate gaps, the credited answer addresses one of them while a tempting wrong answer addresses the other, usually phrased slightly too strongly.

## Principle support

*"which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the reasoning?"*

Find the broad rule that licenses the specific move the argument makes, essentially a sufficient assumption written at a general level. The standard trap is a plausible-sounding principle that actually describes a slightly different situation than the one in the stimulus.

## Evaluate

*"which of the following would be most useful to know in evaluating the argument?"*

Rare, and solved by testing each answer choice with both a "yes" and a "no": if neither answer changes how strong the argument seems, that choice is wrong. If a yes and a no genuinely swing the argument's strength in opposite directions, that's the credited answer.
