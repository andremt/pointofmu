This is a lookup reference, not a read-once primer, the tags here are the exact vocabulary the LSAT question tracker skill uses when logging questions, so a `flaw` question or a `too-strong` trap means the same thing here as it does in the log. Come back to this page whenever you're diagnosing a miss and want the precise tag rather than a vague description.

## Wrong-answer traps

Wrong answers aren't wrong at random, the test writers take the credited answer and alter it in a small number of recurring ways. Naming the alteration turns "that one felt off" into a repeatable elimination skill.

**Degree**
- `too-strong`, overstates. Words like *all, never, must, only, cannot, always, impossible, proves*. Common on necessary assumption (which usually needs something modest) and inference (weaker claims are easier to prove). Don't eliminate on strong language reflexively though, a strong answer is correct when the text itself is absolute.
- `too-weak`, so hedged it does nothing. Common on sufficient assumption and strengthen, where a "may possibly contribute" answer fails to do the required work.

**Scope**
- `out-of-scope`, introduces something the text never discussed. Usually feels reasonable, which is exactly why it's tempting, it's the kind of thing a knowledgeable person would say about the topic.
- `narrow-scope`, true, but too small for the question asked. The classic main-point trap: accurately describes one paragraph when the question asked about the whole passage.
- `half-scope`, comparative-passage specific. Accurately describes one of the two passages when the question asked about both.
- `comparison-trap`, invents a comparison the text doesn't support (e.g., "more than they used to," "more than the other group," when the text never compared the two).

**Interpretation**
- `opposite`, states the reverse of what the text says. Deceptively easy to miss, especially when a negation is buried mid-sentence.
- `reversed`, logic-specific: reverses a conditional relationship or flips the direction of causation. The content is right, the direction is wrong.
- `half-right`, the first clause is perfect, the second is wrong (or vice versa). The most common trap on long answer choices, since attention relaxes once the opening reads correctly.
- `word-match`, reuses distinctive language from the text while saying something the text didn't. Especially punishing on RC Identification questions, where the credited answer is usually heavily paraphrased.

**Relevance**
- `true-but-irrelevant`, accurate and supported, but does nothing for the actual task. Often a restatement of a premise on a strengthen/weaken question.
- `so-what`, flaw-specific. Describes something the argument genuinely does, but that thing isn't actually a flaw.
- `did-not-happen`, flaw-specific. Names a real flaw type in convincing abstract language, but this particular argument doesn't commit it.
- `premise-challenge`, attacks a premise instead of the reasoning. Automatically wrong on any assumption-family question, since premises are given as true.
- `real-world-knowledge`, true in the world, unsupported by the text. A liability on RC science and law passages specifically, the test is whether it's *in the passage*, not whether it's actually correct.

**Two habits that make this vocabulary pay off:** work wrong-to-right, eliminate the four you can name a defect in rather than hunting for the one that's right, and defer judgment until you've read all five, since answer (A) looking good is often the setup for answer (D) looking better.

## Skill gaps (the root-cause diagnosis)

The gap field is what drill prescriptions key off, so it's worth the most thought of any tag. The question to ask on every miss: *what would have had to be different for me to get this right?*

**LR gaps**
- `core-id`, misidentified the conclusion or the supporting premise.
- `scope-shift`, missed the new term that appears in the conclusion but not the premises.
- `conditional`, conditional-logic error: illegal reversal, illegal negation, bad chaining, or confusing necessary with sufficient.
- `causal`, missed an alternative cause, reverse causation, or coincidence.
- `quantifier`, some/most/all confusion, or percent-vs-amount confusion.
- `task-confusion`, read the stem wrong and did the wrong job (strengthened when asked to weaken, etc).
- `flaw-id`, saw the gap in the argument but couldn't name the flaw, or named the wrong one.
- `answer-abstraction`, couldn't decode an abstractly-worded answer choice.
- `prephrase`, went straight to the answers without predicting first, and got pulled around by them.
- `elimination`, got it down to two finalists and picked the wrong one.
- `degree`, missed that an answer was too strong or too weak.
- `premise-attack`, attacked a premise instead of the reasoning.
- `negation-test`, didn't run it (or ran it wrong) on a necessary-assumption question.

**RC gaps**
- `scale`, didn't identify the passage's central debate, or built the wrong one.
- `author-position`, misplaced the author on the Scale, or misjudged how strongly they hold their view.
- `structure`, passage map unclear; couldn't say what each paragraph was actually doing.
- `locate`, knew what was needed but couldn't find the supporting text in time.
- `inference-leap`, inferred past what the text actually supports.
- `scope-match`, picked an answer that was true but too narrow, or too broad, for the question asked.
- `detail-retention`, over-read details on the first pass, or had to reread heavily to find them again.

**Shared gaps**
- `timing`, process was sound, pace wasn't.
- `careless`, genuinely knew it, misread or misclicked. Be skeptical of this one, it's the most over-used tag in any LSAT tracker, because it's the least uncomfortable explanation. If the real answer is "I read the conclusion as the last sentence," that's `core-id`, not carelessness.
- `unfamiliar-content`, the subject matter itself blocked comprehension.
- `other`, nothing above fits; put the detail in the note.

## What the numbers are for

None of this vocabulary matters unless it's applied the same way every time, a `flaw` question logged in August has to mean the same thing as one logged in April for the accumulated data to say anything real. When in doubt about which tag applies, the tracker skill's own guidance is to pick the closest fit and use `other` with a note rather than inventing a new tag on the spot.
