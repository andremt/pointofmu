RC rewards reading for **structure and opinion**, not for detail. Details can always be looked up once a question asks about them; structure can't be reconstructed under time pressure after the fact. Almost every RC miss traces back to a first read that captured facts instead of architecture.

## The Scale

Nearly every LSAT passage is organized around a tension, two competing views, an old consensus against a new finding, a criticism and a defense. That tension is the **Scale**. Find it, place the author on it, and most questions become answerable without rereading the passage from scratch.

Two moves, in order:
1. **Read *for* the Scale** in the first paragraph or two, identify the central debate and who holds each position.
2. **Read *through* the Scale** for everything after, ask what each new chunk of text is doing *relative to* that debate: supporting one side, undercutting the other, providing background, qualifying an earlier claim.

Two properties worth tracking as you read: **balance** (if the Scale is lopsided, one side gets a sentence, the other gets three paragraphs, the credited main-point answer will reference only the heavy side, and expecting a balanced answer where the passage itself is unbalanced produces a predictable wrong choice), and the **author's position and its intensity** (not just which side the author favors, but how strongly, the spectrum runs disgust, dislike, slight disfavor, neutrality, slight favor, like, endorsement; wrong answers routinely get the side right and the intensity wrong).

## PEAR

The active-reading loop, run at every paragraph break and more often through a dense opening:

- **Pause** at the end of the paragraph, don't let the passage go by unprocessed.
- **Evaluate** what that paragraph just did. Which side of the Scale? Whose opinion? Does the Scale need revising?
- **Anticipate** where the passage is going next. Most predictions will be wrong, and that's fine, the act of predicting is what keeps attention from drifting, and drift is what forces rereading later.
- **Reread**, but only when the Pause reveals you genuinely didn't follow something load-bearing. Rereading a detail you merely don't remember wastes time you can spend looking it up when a question actually asks for it.

## A minimal passage map

One line per paragraph, naming its **function**, not its content:

```
P1: old consensus on X + hint author disagrees
P2: new evidence undercutting it
P3: objection from traditionalists
P4: author's rebuttal via analogy
```

Function beats content because most questions ask what a paragraph *does*, not what it's *about*, noting content is a way of feeling productive while missing what's actually being tested.

## The three question categories

**Identification** (*"According to the passage..."*), the answer is stated in the text, usually 1-2 per passage. The difficulty is finding it, since the correct answer is typically paraphrased heavily; scanning for matching vocabulary fails here, and is itself the classic `word-match` trap. Go find the actual text, never answer from memory.

**Inference** (*"It can be inferred..." / "the author would most likely agree..."*), the most common category, 2-5 per passage. Find the relevant text first, then infer from it, inferring from memory rather than the actual passage is where most RC misses originate. The inferential step must be valid but may be genuinely unexpected; correct answers stay close to the text, while tempting wrong answers are the reasonable-sounding ones that require one extra assumption the text doesn't support.

**Synthesis** (*"main idea" / "primary purpose" / "function of the third paragraph"*), tests the whole passage, or one part relative to the whole. A solid Scale and passage map answers these without needing to reread. The dominant trap is an answer that's **true but too narrow**, accurately describing one paragraph when the question asked about the whole passage. Watch especially for a passage with a short "curved tail" (an application or prediction in the final lines), that tail is rarely the actual main point.

## Comparative passages

Two shorter passages instead of one, with questions overwhelmingly about the **relationship** between them. Read the second passage explicitly asking how it relates to the first: agree, disagree, broader scope, narrower scope, a different question entirely, or the same conclusion reached by a different route. The signature trap is the **half-scope answer**, accurately describing one passage when the question asked about both, and it's the single most common wrong answer on comparative sets precisely because half of it is genuinely correct.

## Timing

Roughly 8-9 minutes per passage-and-questions across four passages in 35 minutes. Spending 3-4 minutes on the passage itself is normal and correct, a good first read makes every question fast, while a rushed read turns every question into its own rereading exercise. If a passage is genuinely impenetrable, the questions are still worth attempting: Identification questions in particular are often gettable even off a weak read, since the answer is sitting in the text regardless of how well you followed the argument.
