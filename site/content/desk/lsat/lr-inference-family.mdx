This family requires a genuine shift in reading mode. The stimulus is a set of stated facts, not an argument, there's no conclusion, no premises, no gap to attack. Reading for an "argument core" here is actively the wrong instinct, and it's usually what's happening when an inference question feels harder than it should.

## Inference

*"which one of the following must be true?" / "most strongly supported"*

Combine the given statements to reach the answer. The exact phrasing matters: *must be true* demands airtight logical proof from the given facts, while *most strongly supported* only needs the best-supported answer among the choices, not certainty.

Correct answers on inference questions tend to be **weak**, a modest, narrow claim is much easier to prove airtight than a sweeping one. This is the opposite instinct from the Assumption family, where a necessary assumption also tends to be modest but for a different reason, and that reversal (expecting a strong, satisfying answer the way you would on strengthen/weaken) is exactly what catches people here. Strong language is a bigger red flag on inference than almost anywhere else in the section.

The most frequent source of correct answers: chaining two or more conditional statements together, or combining a some/most statement with a conditional to get a valid, if narrow, conclusion.

## Principle example

A principle is stated in general form; find the specific case that conforms to it (or, on some variants, the case that violates it). This is really applied conditional logic, check that the case actually satisfies the principle's trigger condition, and that the outcome described actually follows from the principle given that trigger.

## Explain the result

*"which one of the following, if true, most helps to explain the discrepancy?"*

Two facts are presented that sit in apparent tension. Find the additional fact that makes both of the given facts true at the same time. The credited answer never disputes either given fact, an answer that undercuts one of them is wrong by construction, because the question already told you both facts are true; your job is to explain how, not to pick a side.
