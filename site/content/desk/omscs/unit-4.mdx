## The four ingredients, every time

Every Bayesian inference problem has the same skeleton:

- **Prior**, π(θ), your belief about the parameter before seeing data.
- **Likelihood**, f(x|θ), how likely the observed data is, as a function of θ.
- **Posterior**, π(θ|x) ∝ π(θ) · f(x|θ), belief after seeing data, proportional to prior times likelihood.
- **Evidence**, f(x), the normalizing constant that makes the posterior integrate to 1. You often don't need to compute it explicitly if you only care about the *shape* of the posterior.

That proportionality sign is doing a lot of work: most of Bayesian computation is figuring out what distribution "prior × likelihood" is proportional to, without ever computing the messy normalizing integral.

## Conjugate families: why some priors are special

A prior is **conjugate** to a likelihood if the resulting posterior is the same *family* of distribution as the prior, just with updated parameters. The three you'll use constantly:

- **Beta-Binomial**: Beta(α, β) prior on a probability, binomial data → posterior is Beta(α + successes, β + failures). The update is just addition.
- **Gamma-Poisson**: Gamma(α, β) prior on a rate, Poisson data → posterior is Gamma(α + Σx, β + n).
- **Normal-Normal**: Normal prior on a mean, normal data with known variance → posterior is Normal, with the posterior mean a precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean.

Conjugacy isn't just a computational convenience, it also builds intuition, because in every case the posterior parameters are literally "prior parameters plus a summary of the data." Once you've derived the Beta-Binomial update by hand once, you can see the same additive pattern everywhere else.

## From a posterior to an answer

A posterior distribution isn't itself the deliverable, you still have to extract something usable from it:

- **Point estimation**: posterior mean, median, or mode, depending on what loss function you're implicitly minimizing.
- **Credible intervals**: the Bayesian analogue of a confidence interval, but with the "95% probability the parameter is in here" interpretation Unit 2 established. The two common constructions are equal-tailed intervals and the highest posterior density (HPD) interval, which is narrower when the posterior is skewed.
- **Hypothesis testing**: computing the posterior probability of a hypothesis directly, rather than a p-value against a null.
- **Prediction**: the posterior predictive distribution folds in your remaining parameter uncertainty when predicting a new observation, which is why Bayesian predictive intervals tend to be a bit wider than a naive plug-in estimate would give you.

## Choosing priors honestly

Prior choice runs from fully informative (elicited from a domain expert or earlier data) to deliberately non-informative (flat or nearly flat, letting the data dominate). A useful way to think about a prior's strength is its **effective sample size**, a Beta(2,2) prior behaves like having already seen 4 observations before your real data arrives, while a Beta(20,20) behaves like 40. That framing makes it much easier to judge whether a proposed prior is reasonable for the problem at hand, or accidentally overwhelming a small dataset.

**Empirical Bayes** is the practice of estimating the prior's own parameters from the data itself, rather than fixing them in advance. It's a pragmatic middle ground, not fully Bayesian in the purist sense (the prior is supposed to come from outside the data), but often a reasonable way to get a sensible, data-informed prior when no strong external information exists.
